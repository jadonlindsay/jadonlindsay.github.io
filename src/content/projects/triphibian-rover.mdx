## The Problem

Drones burn most of their power just staying in the air, which means short missions. A bigger battery doesn't fix it, because the added weight eats most of the gain. A drone that could *drive* when it doesn't need to fly could stay out far longer, which matters most where people shouldn't go: extreme search and rescue, or inspecting unstable construction sites. More runtime means a slower, more careful search.

A classmate pitched the concept to Aggies Create, and it was selected for funding out of several pitches. We started with the idea and nothing else. No reference design existed for a vehicle that flies, drives, *and* floats, so every part of it had to be worked out from first principles.

## Getting It to Drive

We explored three ways to move on the ground:

- **Skis on stilts,** pushed along by rotor thrust. Simple, but the drone would just get blown around with almost no control.
- **Tank treads,** which could handle rough terrain and hide a buoyant core, but they were heavy, mechanically complex, and blocked airflow under the props.
- **Foam wheels,** which solved three problems at once: they float, they absorb landing and rough-terrain impacts, and their bladed profile pushes through water.

## The Frame

Running a drive axle through the body meant a custom **H-frame** instead of the standard X-frame. That ruled out off-the-shelf frames, so every structural part had to be designed and 3D printed in-house. The rover drives and steers on two motors using differential wheel speed.

## Sizing for Buoyancy

Closed-cell foam wheels keep the rover afloat. Neutral buoyancy for a 2 kg design load meant about 2 L of displaced water, so I targeted about 4 L total (61 in³ per wheel) to keep the wheels only partly submerged. The final rover is about 0.9 kg, so the wheels ride roughly 45% submerged, with plenty of margin.

## Optimizing the Wheel

A teammate's Ansys study picked the best wheel shape out of four candidates. My job was to refine it without losing buoyancy.

<Gallery section="wheel" />

The wheel has three sections: a solid cylinder at the center, a drafted cone on one side, and extruded paddle blades on the other. Every dimension trades against the others, because the wheel has to hold 61 in³ to float.

- **A volume-locked model:** I built the SolidWorks model on global variables and equations, so changing one input automatically resized the cylinder to hold volume at 61 in³. Every variant had identical buoyancy, so each design point was an apples-to-apples drag comparison.
- **Working around the software:** SolidWorks Flow Simulation can't model a water surface, so I used a half-model with a symmetry plane at the waterline and treated the results as relative comparisons.

<Gallery section="wheel-final" />

<Video section="flow" />

I ran three staged parametric studies, locking in each result before starting the next:

**1. Draft angle (0°–60°, 7 design points).** Drag fell steadily as the angle increased, but every added degree cut the cone's volume, forcing a thicker cylinder elsewhere to keep the wheel buoyant. I settled on 40° as the balance between drag and volume.

<Chart data="rover-draft-angle" />

**2. Cone height (0.1"–2.4", 13 design points).** A taller cone streamlines the wheel but takes volume from the cylinder, so efficiency drops at both extremes. Drag bottomed out around 1", **~17% lower than the worst case** (0.1").

<Chart data="rover-cone-height" featured />

**3. Paddle depth (0.2"–1.5", 14 design points).** Deeper paddles push more water but add drag almost linearly. 1.5" paddles had 56% more drag than 0.5". Drag alone would favor the shallowest blade, so this was a thrust-vs-drag tradeoff: I chose **0.5"**, estimated to give enough thrust for a slow-moving rover at only ~15% more drag than the 0.2" minimum. Real thrust will be confirmed in a tank test.

<Chart data="rover-paddle-depth" />

## Designing for the Printer

Every part had to print reliably on an FDM printer, so I split large parts to fit the print bed, oriented them for strength, added fillets, and avoided steep overhangs and unsupported features.

## Iterating the Frame

- **Rev 1:** The wheel motors didn't fit, so I resized the drive bays.
- **Rev 2:** It was too short, so the props clipped. I raised the rotor height.
- **Rev 3:** The prop motor screw holes sat under the motor once it was installed, so I relocated them for assembly access.
- **Rev 4:** The battery was loose in its housing, so I tightened the fit.

## Leading the Team

I co-direct the mechanical side: setting weekly priorities, assigning tasks across the team, and keeping the project on track against our Gantt schedule. I owned the tube frame design myself and delegated the electrical housing to a teammate, then reviewed that design to make sure it integrated with the frame.

## What's Next

- **Drive gearing:** The wheel motors spin far too fast to drive the wheels directly; at that speed the blade tips would cavitate in water. Paddle wheels work at roughly 100–300 rpm, so we're working out a reduction of about 150:1 to 400:1.
- **Heat:** Sealing the body for water traps motor heat, and managing that is our next design focus.
- **Sealing:** Wire pass-throughs from the motors to the battery will be sealed with epoxy.
- **Water test:** Measure actual paddle thrust in a tank test.
